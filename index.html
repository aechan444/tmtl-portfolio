PDFIntroduction

As a group of future mathematics teachers, we believe that technology plays an important role in improving the teaching and learning of mathematics. It provides opportunities to make abstract mathematical concepts easier to understand through visual representations, interactive activities, simulations, and digital learning tools. With the help of technology, learners can explore mathematical patterns, solve problems, test their ideas, and develop critical thinking and problem-solving skills. It can also encourage active participation and collaboration by allowing learners to engage in meaningful mathematical activities. Through the effective use of technology, we believe that mathematics can become more engaging, understandable, and relevant to learners' everyday lives.

Through our course, Technology for Teaching and Learning in Mathematics, we have gained a deeper understanding of how technology can support mathematical thinking and improve classroom instruction. We learned that digital tools should be selected based on the lesson objectives, learners' needs, and the resources available in the classroom. We also recognized that technology should not replace the teacher's guidance or the learners' own reasoning but should serve as a tool for exploring concepts and developing a deeper understanding. Furthermore, we realized that effective technology integration requires careful planning, creativity, and consideration of possible challenges, such as limited internet access, unavailable devices, and differences in learners' abilities. These insights have helped us understand that technology is most effective when it is used purposefully and responsibly to support meaningful learning experiences.

This portfolio compilation presents our learning experiences, activities, reflections, and insights into the effective integration of technology in mathematics education. It showcases our understanding of how technological tools can help learners visualize mathematical concepts, investigate patterns, communicate their ideas, and develop strategies for solving problems. As a group, we recognize the importance of creating inclusive and learner-centered environments where technology supports different learning needs and encourages active participation. We aim to apply the knowledge and skills we have gained by choosing appropriate tools, providing alternative learning resources when needed, and guiding learners toward understanding mathematical processes rather than simply obtaining correct answers.

Ultimately, we believe that technology can support mathematical thinking by helping learners become more curious, confident, independent, and critical thinkers who appreciate the value of mathematics in real-life situations.

Learning Experience & Role Play

Two linked outputs. First, we analyzed and redesigned an existing Grade 7 lesson. Then we taught a lesson on misleading graphs through a classroom role play.

Part 1 · Learning Experience Analysis & Redesign

“Fraction–Decimal Match and Explain”

Grade 7 Mathematics: Fractions and Decimals. Based on DepEd Mathematics 7, Quarter 1 Module 5, Expressing Rational Numbers from Fraction Form to Decimal Form and Vice-Versa. We took the module's “What's More” conversion drills and redesigned them so learners work in pairs to predict, match, justify, and only then formalize the rule.

3/4=75/100=0.75

fraction · place value · decimal, plus a verbal explanation that they are the same quantity

  1. Elicit prior thinkingReview fractions, decimals, place value, and GCF.
  2. Predict & matchPairs match fraction and decimal cards before calculating.
  3. Guided explorationConvert using the module procedure and a place-value chart.
  4. Discuss & compareExplain why 3/4 becomes 75/100 and 0.75; correct a wrong solution.
  5. FormalizeWrite the two conversion procedures in their own words.
  6. Apply & transferSolve new problems, including a 4/5-mile context problem.
  7. Exit ticketFive items: 2 fraction→decimal, 2 decimal→fraction, 1 error analysis.
  • Learning theoryConstructivist and experiential learning, with pair discussion
  • Technology roleOptional calculator or spreadsheet, used only to check decimal approximations
  • Offline-readyPrinted cards, place-value chart, and worksheet. No internet needed
  • InclusionLarge-print cards, short directions, peer support, many ways to show understanding
  • PDFLearning Experience: Analysis & RedesignContext, 9-lens analysis, lesson plan, design justification

Part 2 · Role Play

Misleading Digital Media Graphs

A graph of the average monthly temperature in New Haven, Connecticut shows only January to July, so the temperature seems to keep rising. The students notice the missing months, then rebuild the graph with all twelve and compare how the interpretation changes.

“Is the graph showing all the relevant data?”

Intended learning outcomes

  1. Identify misleading features in digital media graphs and explain how they can affect people's interpretation of data.
  2. Collaborate with peers to analyze, discuss, and correct misleading graphs using appropriate statistical concepts.
  3. Communicate their interpretation of statistical information clearly.

Scene flow

  1. Teacher introduction
  2. Presenting the graph
  3. Spotting the misleading data
  4. Recreating the graph
  5. Presenting the new graph
  6. Closing: why correct graphs matter

Constraints and teacher responses

ConstraintTeacher response
Limited or unsuitable resourcesSelect accessible tools that align with lesson objectives.
Limited devices, internet, or electricityProvide printed worksheets and in-class work time.
Risk of technology dependenceRequire step-by-step solutions and peer explanations.
Uncertain learning gainsCompare assessment results and check independent problem-solving.
Difficulty visualizing conceptsUse interactive tools to explore mathematical relationships.

Proposed improvement

Introduce a short low-technology warm-up before digital activities.

  1. Start on paperSketch a graph or solve a basic problem independently.
  2. Explore digitallyUse Desmos or DeltaMath to test ideas and receive feedback.
  3. Compare resultsIdentify similarities, differences, and possible errors.
  4. Explain reasoningJustify answers without relying only on the tool.

Lessons for beginning mathematics teachers

  • Align tools with objectives. Use technology for a clear instructional purpose.
  • Plan for accessibility. Prepare non-digital alternatives and equitable participation.
  • Prioritize reasoning. Ask students to explain and show steps.
  • Evaluate learning. Use assessments, not engagement alone, to judge impact.
  • Keep developing. Build confidence with educational tools and adaptable methods.

Our team's conclusionTechnology becomes truly meaningful when it is used purposefully, responsibly, and inclusively to enhance learning rather than simply complete classroom activities.

Reflection

Technology integration goes beyond devices

Technology integration in education is often understood as simply using computers, gadgets, and online applications during classroom discussions. However, technology integration goes beyond the use of digital tools. It involves selecting and applying appropriate technologies to make teaching more effective, meaningful, and accessible to learners.

Understanding the different foundational terms in technology provides a broader perspective on how technology supports education. Each concept serves a different purpose, from communicating information and delivering instruction to providing learning resources and improving students' understanding. It also emphasizes that effective learning does not always require advanced devices or an internet connection. Even simple technological resources can contribute to meaningful learning when used appropriately.

In mathematics education, technology can help students understand abstract concepts through visual representations, interactive activities, and practical applications. However, the effectiveness of technology depends on how well it supports the learning objectives and addresses students' needs. Teachers must carefully choose tools that encourage participation, understanding, and critical thinking rather than simply making lessons more entertaining.

“Overall, technology integration is not about using the most advanced tools but about using available resources with purpose and creativity to create meaningful learning experiences.”
